This model was trained using our own original image dataset. It predicts whether a facial expression is closer to happy or sad based on the examples we provided in Teachable Machine.

Project Overview

For this project, we used Google Teachable Machine to train an image classification model using original photos contributed by both group members. We organized our dataset into two classes: Happy and Sad.

Our goal was to explore how machine learning models recognize patterns in facial expressions, while also thinking critically about bias, training data, and the limits of AI systems.

Dataset Summary

  • 2 classes: Happy and Sad
  • 100 original images contributed by each group member
  • Images combined by class, not by person
  • Variation in angle, lighting, background, and facial expression

Project Statement

Overview

For our project, we decided to classify images of people based on emotion, whether they are displaying happiness or sadness based on their facial expressions. Although it seems simple, these emotions are broad and people show these emotions in vastly different ways, and we thought it would be interesting to see how the model performs with different facial expressions that could technically fit into either of these two emotion categories.

Our project is also helping us display our AI and web development skills, so that we can efficiently train a model and host it on our website. However, our AI skills aren’t only technical, as this project will also help us explore how AI is easily susceptible to bias. Throughout the project, we relied on examples from Dr. Joy Buolamwini’s book Unmasking AI to help guide our thinking.

Data

In our project, we had about 50 pictures of each emotion from each of us to train the model, labeled in our repository as AleeyaHappy/Sad and ChelseaHappy/Sad, with one folder each for Happy and Sad. Most of the pictures are of us creating the project, but there are also a few different people represented in the dataset, with different identities they hold.

The pictures themselves also have different characteristics, such as different lighting, different settings, and different image quality. There are some filler pictures from each of us in order to make sure we had enough pictures to meet the project requirements and train the model, but these do not make up the entire dataset. Having different faces and faces in different scenarios was something that we wanted in the dataset so the model could be better equipped to see how different people show happiness.

Our selection of people is limited because we needed permission from people to be in a public dataset that would be visible to members of our class. Although we were able to control this in our own project, we know that this often is not the case in the real world when people’s data is used to train AI systems.

As we saw in Unmasking AI, when the owners of the Atlantic Towers apartment building tried to install facial recognition for entry into the building, data collection is often done without the choice of the people whose data is being collected. At the same time, we also know that our model may have been more accurate for more people if we had increased the number of people represented in the dataset. Only training our model on a few people may mean that it will not work well on others, especially people whose identities are not already reflected in the dataset.

Process

We used Google’s Teachable Machine as shown in the lecture materials and uploaded our samples into the system to train the model. When we initially trained the model, we found that it was not very accurate and often gave a higher confidence score for sad even when we tested it with clearly happy facial expressions.

We think one reason for this is that the sad dataset only included pictures with one person, while the happy dataset included more pictures with multiple people. Because of that, the model may have started associating the number of people in the image with the emotion itself, instead of focusing only on the facial expression.

Lessons Learned

Through using our model, we were able to see bias show up in a very direct way. Our dataset was small and limited in scope, which made it much harder for the model to accurately classify a displayed emotion. This connects to the first examples of facial recognition that Dr. Joy Buolamwini shares in Unmasking AI, where she faced difficulty getting facial recognition software to even detect her face.

The model in the CREATURES code library discussed in the book likely had a limited training dataset that did not represent many different people with different identities, so it did not accurately reflect the population of users who would interact with that technology. Having a limited dataset can lead to bias and even cause whole identities to be ignored by the model.

This project helped us understand that AI is not neutral. A model is only as good as the data it learns from. If the dataset is narrow, inconsistent, or biased, the model can learn patterns that do not generalize well. Even a simple facial-expression classifier reflects choices made during data collection, labeling, and testing. With more representative data, the model could become more accurate and more inclusive.
