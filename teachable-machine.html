Project Overview

For this project, we used Google Teachable Machine to train an image classification model using original photos contributed by both group members. We organized our dataset into two classes: Happy and Sad.

Our goal was to explore how machine learning models recognize patterns in facial expressions, while also thinking critically about bias, training data, and the limits of AI systems.

Dataset Summary

  • 2 classes: Happy and Sad
  • 100 original images contributed by each group member
  • Images combined by class, not by person
  • Variation in angle, lighting, background, and facial expression

Project Statement

Overview

For our project, we decided to classify images of people based on emotion, whether they are displaying happiness or sadness based on their facial expressions. Although it seems simple, these emotions are broad and different people show these emotions in vastly different ways, and we thought it would be interesting to see how the model performs with different facial expressions that could technically fit into either of these two emotion categories.

Our project is also helping us display our AI and web development skills, so that we can efficiently train a model and host it on our website. However, our AI skills aren't only technical, as this project will also help us explore how AI is easily susceptible to bias. Throughout the project, we relied on examples from Dr. Joy Buolawmini's book Unmasking AI to help us guide our thinking. Dr. Buolawmini, in her book, describes three questions that facial recognition often attempts to answer: is this a face, what kind of face is this, and have I seen this face before. Trying to detect emotion would fall into that second question, and the technical term that Dr. Buolawmini mentions explicitly for this type of detection is “affect recognition”. We recognize that it could be easy to “trick” the model by showing an expression that doesn't actually match someone's emotion (such as smiling when you are actually sad), but we were still interested in seeing if the model could still recognize and accurately sort the common facial expressions for happiness and sadness.

Data

In our project, we had about 50 pictures of each emotion from each of us to train the model, labeled in our repository as AleeyaHappy/Sad and ChelseaHappy/Sad (with one folder each for Happy and Sad). Most of the pictures are of us that are creating the project, but there are a few different people represented in the pictures in our dataset, with different identities they hold.

The pictures themselves also have different characteristics, such as different lighting, different settings, and different image quality. There are some “filler” pictures from each of us in order to make sure we had enough pictures, but these do not comprise the entire dataset. Having different faces/faces in different scenarios was something that we wanted to have to train our model so it could be better equipped to see how different people show happiness and sadness.

Our selection of people is limited due to getting permission from people to be in the dataset used to train the model, because of the fact that the data is public to members of our class. Although we were able to control this here, we know that this is (often) not the case in the world in other instances where our data is used to train other AI models or for other functions.

As we saw in Unmasking AI when the owners of the Atlantic Towers apartment building tried to install facial recognition for entry into the building, data collection is often done without the choice of those whose data is being collected. On the other hand, we know that our model may be more accurate for more people had we increased the number of people included in our dataset for training. Only training our model on a few people may mean the model won't work well on other people, especially other people that hold identities not already reflected in the dataset. We were able to get permission from a few more people as we worked on the final iteration, but realistically, adding only a few more people to our dataset may not necessarily create any drastic changes in the accuracy of the model.

Process

We used Google's Teachable Machine as shown in the lecture materials, and uploaded our samples to the Teachable Machine to train the model. Some of the samples' file formats as originally uploaded to GitHub did not work for the Teachable Machine, so they were omitted for the first iteration of the project, and reuploaded for the second iteration. When we initially trained the model, we found that it was not accurate, and gave a higher confidence score for sadness when we tested with classic happy facial expressions.

Initially, we think that a reason for this is that the sad dataset only includes pictures with one person, where the happy dataset includes pictures with multiple people, so the model classifies images with one person as sad regardless of their facial expression. It was also surprising to see this happen when we tested even though our pictures are the most represented in the dataset. Another issue that we noticed was that the classifier was also paying attention to how far someone was from the camera, where a closer shot was classified more often as sad, and a further away shot was classified as happy, regardless of actual facial expressions.

We trained a second model on more data that included more people and photos more cropped to a face to try and correct these issues, but the model was still classifying happy faces as sad. The distance away from the camera seemed to make less of a difference, but the presence of other objects in the photo would skew the results as happy regardless of facial expressions.

We decided to proceed with a third iteration of the model, where we made more targeted adjustments to the dataset to make sure the results would be more accurate. We uploaded more cropped or zoomed in images to try to focus more on the facial expressions in order to emphasize smiles and frowns. We also worked on balancing the dataset further by including more people in the sad category rather than having it be mostly of one person. The goal was to get the model to focus more on actual facial expressions instead of things like background, distance or the number of people in the image. It turned out that this helped a bit, although the model was still inconsistent, which shows how hard it is to get a classification of emotion right.

Lessons Learned/Reflection On Our Project

Through using our model, we saw bias come through, which reinforced the idea from Unmasking AI that technology is not neutral. Our dataset was small, and very limited in scope, which makes it much more difficult for the model to accurately classify a displayed emotion. This is related to the first couple of examples of facial recognition that Dr. Buolawmini shares in Unmasking AI, where she faced difficulty in getting facial recognition software to even detect her face.

The model in the CREATURES code library, as used in the technology mentioned in the book, very likely had a limited dataset that did not represent many different people with different identities, and thus did not accurately represent the population of users that would be using this library. Having a limited dataset will inevitably lead to bias and even whole identities being ignored from the model's results.

With significantly more data, the model could possibly be more accurate and more representative of the population. However, this also introduces a trade off since it may be difficult to get permission from a large number of people, which then raises the question of whether it is possible to make an ethical, yet accurate, model.

Working through our project also reinforced the idea that these results should absolutely not be taken at face value only and if they are used to make decisions, we should be extremely skeptical about the results. In chapter 6 the book, Dr. Buolawmini mentioned a study where facial recognition was used to predict criminality, and that model only included around eighteen hundred people. While this is absolutely more people than what we have represented in our dataset, it is still far too small to get an accurate read on something as serious as criminality, which could greatly negatively affect many parts of peoples' lives.

If the intent was to determine something related to criminal proceedings that might affect someone's life, this result should absolutely not be the only thing that determines that due to the fact that technology is not neutral. Dr. Buolawmini, in the same chapter, also describes an article where researchers used facial images to determine someone's sexual orientation, which could similarly jeopardize someone's safety and livelihood based on a result that is likely extremely biased. As we mentioned in our first section of this statement, we realize that it is easy to trick the model in principle by just simply showing some facial expression that is different from your actual emotion in the moment. Relying solely or largely on results from a facial recognition model could easily introduce or reinforce inaccuracy and bias, and could very likely harm someone's livelihood.

Even after a third round of improvements, the model was still inconsistent at times, which shows that improving a model is not only about adding more data, but what the data actually looks like and what the model is actually picking up on.
